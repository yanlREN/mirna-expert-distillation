## 2. Response patterns supported by public content

### Pattern A — Begin with the biological recognition problem, then decompose the response

In the Annenberg interview, Carrington frames the high-level problem as how a plant recognizes a virus as foreign, elicits defense and acquires specificity. The same answer then moves from a broad question to experimentally separable stages: recognition, host response, defense output and future resistance. This supports a candidate interrogation habit:

1. define the invading entity and matched host context;
2. identify the proposed recognition event;
3. separate response activation from restriction of infection;
4. test the responsible host component by perturbation;
5. measure viral and host phenotypes separately.

Source: `src-url-annenberg-carrington-genomics-interview`; card `claim-carrington-c-002`.

The pattern is historically explicit but should not be labeled a universal Carrington doctrine until it recurs in additional expert-authored contexts.

### Pattern B — Use genome-scale data to expose surprises, not to finish causality

The interview presents whole-genome expression measurements as a way to see coordinated responses and unexpected candidate genes. It then returns to locus identity, surrounding genomic context, known function and focused experiments. The important methodological boundary is that a differential signal creates a candidate and a question; it does not identify a direct viral target or a causal defense mechanism.

Source: `src-url-annenberg-carrington-genomics-interview`; card `claim-carrington-c-003`.

This is an early-microarray example. Modern small-RNA sequencing additionally requires library, mapping, multimapping, normalization and multiplicity controls that the interview does not discuss.

### Pattern C — Prefer a tractable genetic system when the original host obscures the question

The PNAS profile describes the shift from tobacco-focused virology to Arabidopsis because host genetics could expose mechanisms that were difficult to resolve in the earlier system. It also connects the HC-Pro counterdefense hypothesis to viral genetics and parallel work rather than to a single observation. This supports a model-choice heuristic: choose the organism and perturbation system that makes competing explanations discriminable, then preserve the transfer boundary back to crops or other hosts.

Source: `src-doi-10-1073-pnas-1008044107`; card `claim-carrington-c-004`.

### Pattern D — Convergent phenotype does not require identical suppressor mechanism

The 2004 meeting report says Carrington presented p19, p21 and HC-Pro as viral suppressors that perturb miRNA/siRNA-related regulation, while reported association assays separated p19/p21 from HC-Pro. This is a useful public example of retaining a shared functional class while asking which molecular step each component affects.

Source: `src-doi-10-1261-rna-7900204`; card `claim-carrington-c-005`.

Because the source is a meeting report, this pattern is valid as public framing only. Scientific use must cite standing primary experiments, and later independent work shows that RNA-binding behavior varies across suppressors.

## 3. Uncertainty and boundary language

The usable public sources support the following neutral formulations:

- “The genome-wide result identifies candidates that were not expected; it does not by itself explain why they changed.”
- “The host gene can be tested by matched genetic perturbation and infection, with viral accumulation and plant susceptibility recorded separately.”
- “A viral protein may belong to a suppressor class while acting at a different step from another suppressor.”
- “A model-system result provides a mechanism test in that system; crop or cross-species transfer remains a separate question.”

These are paraphrased methodological implications, not quotations and not statements of Carrington's current private view.

## 4. Recurrent questions for future article interrogation

1. What makes the virus or viral RNA “foreign” in the proposed model, and is recognition directly tested?
2. Is the host factor required for virus restriction, for symptom formation, or for both?
3. Does the perturbation alter viral RNA/protein accumulation before the phenotype changes?
4. Is a genome-wide expression or small-RNA signal used only to nominate candidates, or incorrectly treated as direct mechanism?
5. Are locus identity, assembly, paralogs and genetic background resolved before assigning function?
6. Does a viral suppressor block small-RNA production, duplex availability, AGO loading, slicing, or silencing spread?
7. Are suppressor phenotypes demonstrated during native infection, or only in a transgene reporter assay?
8. Does a conclusion from Arabidopsis transfer to the crop, virus strain, tissue and temperature actually tested?
