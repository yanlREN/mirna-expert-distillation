## 2. Supported mechanisms and qualifications

### 2.1 DCL1–HYL1–SERRATE is a processing module, but mutant phenotypes are not step-specific

The 2002 Chen-team genetics linked DCL1 and HEN1 to miRNA metabolism and development (`src-doi-10-1016-s0960-9822-02-01017-5`). Independent CBC/SERRATE genetics, HYL1 structural-rescue work, and SERRATE phase-separation work support a DCL1–HYL1–SE processing module (`src-doi-10-1073-pnas-0802493105`, `src-doi-10-1016-j-str-2010-02-006`, `src-doi-10-1038-s41556-020-00606-5`; `claim-chen-c-005`, `claim-chen-c-006`).

The qualification is important: SE and the cap-binding complex also affect splicing, and DCL1/HYL1/SE mutants have broad developmental consequences. A lower mature-miRNA level plus a phenotype cannot by itself distinguish MIR transcription, pri-miRNA stability, processing rate/accuracy, loading, action, or turnover. Direct precursor measurements, processing intermediates, interaction/localization evidence, and rescue are needed to locate the step.

### 2.2 HEN1 methylation protects small-RNA 3′ ends, but protection is not the whole turnover pathway

Chen-team biochemical/genetic work established HEN1-dependent terminal methylation and showed that loss of methylation exposes miRNAs and siRNAs to 3′ uridylation (`src-doi-10-1126-science-1107130`, `src-doi-10-1016-j-cub-2005-07-029`). Independent HEN1 kinetic analysis supports efficient modification of duplex RNA and a role for domains outside the catalytic core in substrate interaction (`src-doi-10-1261-rna-2281410`; `claim-chen-c-003`).

Later work shows that tailing and trimming are a network rather than a single linear reaction. HESO1 and URT1 can act redundantly or sequentially, and removal of uridylation can reveal extensive trimming (`src-doi-10-1371-journal-pgen-1005091`); independent URT1 structure/function supports a distinct catalytic architecture (`src-doi-10-1016-j-bbrc-2020-01-124`; `claim-chen-c-004`, `claim-chen-c-008`). Therefore, an isomiR end profile is not automatically a direct readout of one enzyme, and a sequencing abundance change is not by itself a degradation-rate measurement.

### 2.3 Plant miRNA action includes cleavage and translational repression

The miR172/APETALA2 study showed a developmental case in which protein regulation was prominent despite limited change in AP2 RNA (`src-doi-10-1126-science-1088060`). Independent genetics found a widespread translational-inhibitory component separable from slicing (`src-doi-10-1126-science-1159151`), and an independent biochemical system showed AGO1-RISC can inhibit translation initiation without inducing deadenylation or mRNA decay (`src-doi-10-1016-j-molcel-2013-10-033`; `claim-chen-c-002`, `claim-chen-c-007`).

This supports a dual-mode framework, not the claim that every plant miRNA target is mainly translationally repressed. Target, binding-site placement, complementarity, tissue, stage, protein/RNA assay, and AGO context all matter. PARE/degradome cleavage evidence supports cleavage in a matched system but does not exclude translation effects or prove phenotype causality.

### 2.4 AGO loading, localization, and movement are distinct gates

Independent single-cell-type measurements in the root showed spatially patterned AGO loading and activity, including movement-compatible regulatory schemes (`src-doi-10-15252-embj-2018100754`). Independent HASTY work showed regulated cell-to-cell and vascular miRNA movement and argued against a simple animal-like EXPORTIN5 analogy (`src-doi-10-15252-embj-2020107455`). Chen-team work then linked cortical microtubules to non-cell-autonomous action by limiting cytoplasmic AGO1 loading (`src-doi-10-1016-j-devcel-2022-03-015`; `claim-chen-c-009`, `claim-chen-c-010`).

These studies converge on a gate model: production, mobility, AGO loading, target engagement, and phenotype must be tested separately. Detection in a recipient tissue does not alone establish movement from a proposed source; AGO association does not prove a direct target; target repression does not automatically establish the full phenotype.

## 3. Contested or context-dependent points

### Processing bodies versus distributed processing

SERRATE phase separation can promote D-body assembly and processing (`src-doi-10-1038-s41556-020-00606-5`), but the existence or abundance of a visible body is not equivalent to processing flux for every pri-miRNA. A decisive experiment would combine locus-specific nascent transcription, precursor/intermediate quantification, processing-rate measurements, and perturbations that separate phase-separation capacity from protein abundance and catalytic competence.

### Tailing as cause, consequence, or competing branch

HEN1 loss, HESO1/URT1 activity, and trimming phenotypes support coupled end-modification pathways. The 2015 genetic result explicitly exposes redundancy and antagonism (`src-doi-10-1371-journal-pgen-1005091`). An observed U-tail can mark a pathway branch without proving it is the rate-limiting cause of decay. Pulse-chase or metabolic-labeling designs, enzyme-combination mutants, AGO-bound versus free fractions, and matched total/isomiR measurements are needed.

### Translational repression prevalence

Genetic and biochemical evidence makes translational repression real, but prevalence is method-dependent. Protein-sensitive assays can reveal effects missed by RNA-only readouts, while cell-free assays may not reproduce tissue architecture. The safe conclusion is target- and context-specific coexistence of cleavage, RNA decay, and translation control—not a universal ranking.

### Mobility and cell autonomy

The 2019–2022 studies support movement and loading gates, but use different tissues, reporters, and perturbations. Source-restricted expression, grafting or lineage barriers, recipient-cell AGO loading, target effects, and phenotypic rescue should be combined before assigning a mobile miRNA mechanism.

## 4. Species, tissue, and method transfer boundaries

- The corpus is overwhelmingly *Arabidopsis thaliana*. DCL1–HYL1–SE, HEN1, URT1/HESO1, or AGO1 findings cannot be transferred numerically to another plant without checking paralogs, tissue, stage, and assay.
- Plant HASTY should not be described as if the animal Exportin-5 model had simply been copied into plants.
- A family label such as miR165/166 or miR172 does not identify a specific MIR locus, arm, mature sequence, isomiR, or source cell.
- Small-RNA abundance, end heterogeneity, AGO association, target cleavage, protein repression, and developmental phenotype are different observables.
- Raw sequencing data were not downloaded; conclusions use lawful article metadata/abstracts and open full text where available.

## 6. Synthesis handoff

Independent evidence supports four transferable gates: (1) localize a defect among transcription, processing, loading, action, and turnover; (2) interpret HEN1 protection within a redundant tailing/trimming network; (3) measure both RNA cleavage/decay and protein output; and (4) stage movement before loading before direct target action before phenotype. The strongest recurring caution is that a downstream abundance or developmental phenotype does not identify the upstream mechanistic step.
